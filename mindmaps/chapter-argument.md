## Evidence and scope discipline

- Treat the chapter as a documentary-comparative analysis, not as original fieldwork.
- Separate three levels of claim:
  - verified treaty, regulation, institutional, or evaluation evidence;
  - peer-reviewed interpretation of the cases;
  - propositions requiring further empirical testing.
- Preserve the asymmetry between the cases as an analytical fact:
  - the Andean case may be better documented through ex post evaluation and routine committee practice;
  - the Mercosur case requires closer attention to contested implementation, land-use politics, and political responses.
- Do not infer environmental or distributional outcomes from procedural activity alone.
- For each case, trace the chain from rule production to implementation, enforcement, and distribution of costs.
- Keep a claim audit for:
  - negotiating dates and legal status;
  - sustainability provisions and enforcement mechanisms;
  - EUDR obligations and implementation dates;
  - documented effects versus analytical inference.
- State evidence gaps explicitly where monitoring, producer-level data, or local stakeholder evidence is unavailable.

## Evidence-backed refinements from the chapter workspace

- Externalisation is temporal, not a one-off transfer:
  - negotiation;
  - implementation committees;
  - reporting and evaluation cycles;
  - domestic legislation;
  - private certification and administrative guidance.
- The legal text is the starting point; analyse how broad commitments are translated into agendas, requests, monitoring and action.
- Use a controlled qualitative comparison organised around four questions:
  - What sustainability obligations exist?
  - How are they monitored and enforced?
  - What sectoral pressures shape implementation?
  - What legitimacy or contestation do the instruments produce?
- Keep three forms of EU power distinct:
  - regulatory power: defining market conditions;
  - implementation power: monitoring, financing and enforcing them;
  - transformative power: changing production models rather than reallocating compliance costs.
- A model-based Sustainability Impact Assessment projects scenarios; committee activity records institutional process; local or producer evidence is needed to assess lived distributional effects. These forms of evidence are not interchangeable.

## 1. Introduction — 600–700 words

### Externalisation framework

- EU norms are projected through market scale.
- EU rules are not merely bounded by physical territory; rather, the EU's boundaries of governance are "geographically localizable but not necessarily physical" (Lavenex & Schimmelfennig, 2009, p. 805).
- Source: [Lavenex & Schimmelfennig, 2009](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:76475)
- Explain how market access can extend EU governance beyond formal membership.
- Working synthesis from the literature matrix
  - EU market power can turn internal rules into external standards through firms, agreements, and market-access conditions (Bradford, 2020; Damro, 2015).
  - Green integration is contradictory when sustainability claims coexist with uneven implementation capacity and transferred compliance costs.
- Market Power Europe
  - EU market size, institutional capacity, and internal contestation shape external action (Damro, 2015).
  - The Brussels Effect describes how firms adopt EU standards to avoid fragmented production and preserve access to the EU market (Bradford, 2020).
  - Distinguish voluntary corporate convergence from treaty-based obligations and unilateral conditionality.

### Argument

- Green Integration by Contradiction is both:
  - a sustainability-governance project;
  - a legitimacy device for EU external action.
- EU market power can export standards as soft power and governance externalisation (Lavenex & Schimmelfennig, 2009).
- State the counterintuitive claim clearly.
- Legitimation mechanisms
  - discursive: sustainability language presents external action as principled;
  - institutional: TSD committees and domestic advisory groups create visible governance;
  - behavioural: reviews, dialogues, and information-sharing demonstrate activity even where sanctions are weak (Garcia & Gomez Arana, 2025).

### Chapter positioning

- Link EU trade policy to European integration beyond territorial borders.
- Link externalisation to the handbook's sustainability rollback theme.
- Introduce the EU–Andean and EU–Mercosur agreements.

## 2. Conceptual framework — 1,000–1,200 words

### 2.1 Integration by externalisation

- Rules regulate movement and governance between territorial organisations.
- Boundaries can be geographically localizable but not necessarily physical.
- Market size is used to export regulatory standards to countries outside formal EU territory.
- Distinguish externalisation from diffusion, conditionality, and normative power.
- Externalisation unfolds through repeated translation rather than a single legal event.
- Track how rules move from text into committees, reports, domestic measures, certification and guidance.
- Add the difference between:
  - market-based externalisation through firm adaptation;
  - negotiated externalisation through trade agreements;
  - conditional externalisation through preferential access;
  - administrative externalisation through due-diligence and verification systems.
- Audit links
  - [Lavenex & Schimmelfennig, 2009](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:76475)
  - [Damro, 2015, Market Power Europe](https://doi.org/10.1080/13501763.2015.1046903)
  - [Bradford, 2020, The Brussels Effect](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780190088583.001.0001)

### 2.2 Sustainability governance and green colonialism

- Green colonialism refers to the imposition of environmental or sustainability policies by powerful, often Global North, actors in ways that disproportionately burden or disempower communities in the Global South (Claar, 2022, p. 269).
- Use the concept as a critical lens, not as an automatic description of all EU environmental policy.
- Test whether sustainability references create clear, assessable obligations; textual density is not the same as regulatory force (Akdogan, 2025).
- Examine who defines sustainability, who verifies compliance, and who pays for implementation.
- Accountability-by-proxy
  - EU operators and importers transfer verification duties along global supply chains.
  - This may produce top-down compliance without equivalent participation by Southern producers (Gröne & Snopkov, 2025).
- Decolonial corrective
  - Provincialise EU experience.
  - Include local knowledge and non-EU conceptions of sustainability.
  - Treat participation as a substantive condition of legitimacy, not only as consultation (Fisher-Onar & Nicolaïdis, 2022).

### 2.3 Equity impacts and market concentration

- Concentration can undermine equitable access to high-value markets.
- More than 75% of certified products are produced by ten corporate groups (Gröne & Snopkov, 2025; Policy Brief No. 1, 11/2025).
- Analytical question:
  - Do sustainability standards raise environmental performance while narrowing market access for smaller producers?

### 2.4 Compliance burden

- EUDR compliance costs may range from 10–30% of smallholders' income (Judijanto, 2025).
- Source: [Judijanto, 2025, International Journal of Environmental Sciences](https://theaspd.com/index.php/ijes/article/view/1283)
- Verified DOI: [10.64252/yqdekv79](https://doi.org/10.64252/yqdekv79)
- Treat the figure as evidence requiring verification and contextualisation.
- Compare administrative, financial, traceability, and opportunity costs.
- Do not reduce compliance to a cost problem.
  - Ask whether smallholders are excluded from high-value markets.
  - Ask whether corporate concentration follows from certification and data requirements.
  - Ask whether technical assistance changes the distributional outcome.

- Promotional versus assertive enforcement
  - Promotional TSD models rely on dialogue, persuasion, social pressure, and reputation (Marín Durán, 2020).

## 5. Cross-case analysis — 1,200–1,500 words

### 5.1 Asymmetric greening

- Develop the argument comparatively across three dimensions:
  - regulatory asymmetry;
  - procedural asymmetry;
  - distributional asymmetry.
- Ask who defines the standard and who bears implementation costs.
- Compare depoliticised Andean governance with contested Mercosur governance.
- Compare mechanisms, not only outcomes:
  - rule production: EU institutions define or frame standards;
  - implementation: firms, committees, competent authorities, and producers operationalise them;
  - enforcement: sanctions, reputational pressure, dialogue, and market exclusion;
  - distribution: costs and benefits fall unevenly across blocs and within sectors.
- Comparative finding:
  - greener language, architecture, and public justification can coexist with uncertain transformation of extractive export models;
  - the EU can demand compliance more readily than it supplies the finance and policy space needed for socially sustainable compliance.

### 5.2 EU Deforestation Regulation as parallel evidence

- [Explain EUDR scope and requirements]
- [Connect EUDR to both agreements]
- [Assess complementarity and conflict]
- [Assess effects on smallholders, exporters, intermediaries, and corporate groups]
- [Distinguish documented impacts from inference]
- Legal mechanism
  - mandatory due diligence;
  - information collection;
  - risk assessment;
  - risk mitigation;
  - country benchmarking (Regulation (EU) 2023/1115).
- Primary legal source
  - [Consolidated EUDR text, Regulation (EU) 2023/1115](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1115/oj)
  - Relevant products cannot be placed on the EU market unless they are deforestation-free, produced according to relevant country legislation, and covered by a due-diligence statement.
  - Due diligence requires information collection, risk assessment, and risk mitigation.
- Current implementation note
  - The Commission's July 2026 update describes implementation at the end of 2026 and changes to product scope and information tools.
  - [Commission update on EUDR implementation tools, 13 July 2026](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-updates-product-scope-and-tools-support-eudr-2026-07-13_en)
  - Recheck final application dates and product list immediately before submission.
- Governance implication
  - EUDR is a direct market-access condition with extraterritorial effects.
  - It differs from promotional TSD chapters because non-compliant products cannot be placed on the EU market.
- EUDR shows a harder form of externalisation than promotional TSD chapters: autonomous EU law conditions market access without requiring a bilateral agreement.
- Delayed and amended application dates indicate contested implementation or institutional difficulty, not proof that the regulation has failed.
- Evidence to compare
  - legal design;
  - operator responsibility;
  - smallholder readiness;
  - corporate concentration;
  - local coordination and zero-deforestation agreements.

### 5.3 Legitimacy device thesis

- State thesis directly.
- Sustainability provisions may:
  - improve environmental governance;
  - legitimise EU external action;
  - diffuse responsibility for implementation costs.
- Identify conditions under which the thesis is supported.
- Address evidence against overclaiming.
- Observable indicators
  - activity of TSD committees;
  - publication of reviews and meeting minutes;
  - participation of domestic advisory groups;
  - measurable changes in environmental or labour outcomes;
  - access to remedy and enforcement.
- Analytical test
  - If procedural activity expands while material outcomes and enforceability remain weak, the legitimacy-device interpretation gains support.
  - If participation, enforcement, and outcomes improve together, the provisions should be treated as more than symbolic legitimation.
