## 1. Introduction — 600–700 words

### Externalisation framework

- EU norms are projected through market scale.
- EU rules are not merely bounded by physical territory; rather, the EU's boundaries of governance are "geographically localizable but not necessarily physical" (Lavenex & Schimmelfennig, 2009, p. 805).
- Source: [Lavenex & Schimmelfennig, 2009](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:76475)
- Explain how market access can extend EU governance beyond formal membership.
- Working synthesis from the literature matrix
  - EU market power can turn internal rules into external standards through firms, agreements, and market-access conditions (Bradford, 2020; Damro, 2015).
  - Green integration is contradictory when sustainability claims coexist with uneven implementation capacity and transferred compliance costs.
- Market Power Europe
  - EU market size, institutional capacity, and internal contestation shape external action (Damro, 2015).
  - The Brussels Effect describes how firms adopt EU standards to avoid fragmented production and preserve access to the EU market (Bradford, 2020).
  - Distinguish voluntary corporate convergence from treaty-based obligations and unilateral conditionality.

### Argument

- Green Integration by Contradiction is both:
  - a sustainability-governance project;
  - a legitimacy device for EU external action.
- EU market power can export standards as soft power and governance externalisation (Lavenex & Schimmelfennig, 2009).
- State the counterintuitive claim clearly.
- Legitimation mechanisms
  - discursive: sustainability language presents external action as principled;
  - institutional: TSD committees and domestic advisory groups create visible governance;
  - behavioural: reviews, dialogues, and information-sharing demonstrate activity even where sanctions are weak (Garcia & Gomez Arana, 2025).

### Chapter positioning

- Link EU trade policy to European integration beyond territorial borders.
- Link externalisation to the handbook's sustainability rollback theme.
- Introduce the EU–Andean and EU–Mercosur agreements.

## 2. Conceptual framework — 1,000–1,200 words

### 2.1 Integration by externalisation

- Rules regulate movement and governance between territorial organisations.
- Boundaries can be geographically localizable but not necessarily physical.
- Market size is used to export regulatory standards to countries outside formal EU territory.
- Distinguish externalisation from diffusion, conditionality, and normative power.
- Add the difference between:
  - market-based externalisation through firm adaptation;
  - negotiated externalisation through trade agreements;
  - conditional externalisation through preferential access;
  - administrative externalisation through due-diligence and verification systems.
- Audit links
  - [Lavenex & Schimmelfennig, 2009](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:76475)
  - [Damro, 2015, Market Power Europe](https://doi.org/10.1080/13501763.2015.1041411)
  - [Bradford, 2020, The Brussels Effect](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780190088583.001.0001)

### 2.2 Sustainability governance and green colonialism

- Green colonialism refers to the imposition of environmental or sustainability policies by powerful, often Global North, actors in ways that disproportionately burden or disempower communities in the Global South (Claar, 2022, p. 269).
- Use the concept as a critical lens, not as an automatic description of all EU environmental policy.
- Examine who defines sustainability, who verifies compliance, and who pays for implementation.
- Accountability-by-proxy
  - EU operators and importers transfer verification duties along global supply chains.
  - This may produce top-down compliance without equivalent participation by Southern producers (Gröne & Snopkov, 2025).
- Decolonial corrective
  - Provincialise EU experience.
  - Include local knowledge and non-EU conceptions of sustainability.
  - Treat participation as a substantive condition of legitimacy, not only as consultation (Fisher-Onar & Nicolaïdis, 2022).

### 2.3 Equity impacts and market concentration

- Concentration can undermine equitable access to high-value markets.
- More than 75% of certified products are produced by ten corporate groups (Gröne & Snopkov, 2025; Policy Brief No. 1, 11/2025).
- Analytical question:
  - Do sustainability standards raise environmental performance while narrowing market access for smaller producers?

### 2.4 Compliance burden

- EUDR compliance costs may range from 10–30% of smallholders' income (Judijanto, 2025).
- Source: [Judijanto, 2025, International Journal of Environmental Sciences](https://www.theaspd.com/ijes.php)
- Treat the figure as evidence requiring verification and contextualisation.
- Compare administrative, financial, traceability, and opportunity costs.
- Do not reduce compliance to a cost problem.
  - Ask whether smallholders are excluded from high-value markets.
  - Ask whether corporate concentration follows from certification and data requirements.
  - Ask whether technical assistance changes the distributional outcome.

- Promotional versus assertive enforcement
  - Promotional TSD models rely on dialogue, persuasion, social pressure, and reputation (Marín Durán, 2020).

## 5. Cross-case analysis — 1,200–1,500 words

### 5.1 Asymmetric greening

- Develop the argument comparatively across three dimensions:
  - regulatory asymmetry;
  - procedural asymmetry;
  - distributional asymmetry.
- Ask who defines the standard and who bears implementation costs.
- Compare depoliticised Andean governance with contested Mercosur governance.
- Compare mechanisms, not only outcomes:
  - rule production: EU institutions define or frame standards;
  - implementation: firms, committees, competent authorities, and producers operationalise them;
  - enforcement: sanctions, reputational pressure, dialogue, and market exclusion;
  - distribution: costs and benefits fall unevenly across blocs and within sectors.
