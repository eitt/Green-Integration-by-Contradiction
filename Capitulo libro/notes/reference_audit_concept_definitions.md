### Best-Fit Working Definition

`Sustainability gaps` can be defined in this chapter as the documented mismatch between a route's heritage or tourism potential and the governance, community, operational, carrying-capacity, digital, and monitoring conditions required for sustainability in practice.

### Why This Definition Fits

- It matches Liu (2003) and Buckley (2012), who both warn against treating sustainability as a self-evident label.
- It matches Aas et al. (2005), Kabbara et al. (2026), and Liu et al. (2026), which show that coordination and resident roles are necessary conditions, not optional extras.
- It matches Mansourihanis et al. (2026), Zou et al. (2025), and Duarte-Duarte et al. (2021), which treat routes as operational and design problems requiring measurable criteria.
- It matches Kuniyal et al. (2025), which makes carrying-capacity management part of sustainable route practice.
- It fits the chapter logic because the Camino de Lengerke problem is not the absence of heritage meaning, but the uneven documentation of the conditions that would make that meaning sustainable as a route system.

### Editorial Recommendation

If you want the wording to stay very close to the literature, a safer first-use sentence is:

`In this chapter, the term sustainability gaps refers to the set of documented deficits between heritage-route recognition and the sustainability conditions evidenced in better-developed route systems.`

## Heritage Walking Routes

### Verdict

The exact label `Heritage Walking Routes` was not found as a standardized definition in the retained scientific audit. However, the literature base does contain several adjacent constructs that together support the term very well.

### Best-Fit Working Definition

`Heritage Walking Routes` can be defined in this chapter as route-based heritage systems designed, interpreted, or experienced through pedestrian movement, where the path itself forms part of the heritage value, visitor experience, and territorial service network.

### Why This Definition Fits

- It is consistent with Rahman et al. (2025), where the `heritage trail concept` links preservation, identity, local economic benefit, and sustainability.
- It is consistent with Zou et al. (2025), which frames heritage tourism through trail planning rather than isolated sites.
- It is consistent with Grocutt and Wood (2025) and Amaro et al. (2018), which show that route meaning emerges through walking, motivation, wellbeing, and pilgrimage experience.
- It is consistent with Mansourihanis et al. (2026), Akay (2026), and Duarte-Duarte et al. (2021), which show that routes require pedestrian operability and explicit design logic.
- It is consistent with Xie et al. (2025) and Gong et al. (2025), which treat heritage routes as connected systems rather than single monuments.

### Editorial Recommendation

If you want to be especially cautious, define the term once as an umbrella label:

`In this chapter, the term heritage walking routes is used as an umbrella term for pilgrimage routes, heritage trails, heritage corridors, and walkable cultural-tourism routes in which movement along the path is part of the heritage experience itself.`

This keeps the chapter's title language, but makes clear that the term is a synthesis built from prior literature rather than a single canonical definition borrowed verbatim from one source.
